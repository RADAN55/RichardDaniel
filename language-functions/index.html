36-Week Language Functions
K–12 Scope & Sequence for English Learners

Twelve functions · four grade bands · four proficiency tiers · aligned to ELPA21 and Mississippi CCRS-ELA · 2026–2027

Overview & Calendar

Purpose and how to use this

Twelve language functions, taught in order over 36 weeks, give every EL student in K–12 one shared map of what language does — whatever their grade or English level.

A language function is the job a student's language is doing: describing, comparing, explaining why, arguing. Content changes every unit; the function stays constant for three weeks, so students get enough repetitions to own the sentence structures before the next function arrives.

How the document is organized

  • One function per 3-week cycle, 12 functions a year. Each cycle = introduce (week 1), practice across content (week 2), produce and assess (week 3). Functions are sequenced from concrete (describing) to abstract (synthesizing).
  • Four grade bands: K–2, 3–5, 6–8, 9–12. Same function, grade-appropriate content, vocabulary load, and text complexity.
  • Four proficiency tiers inside every band: Newcomer (ELPA21 Level 1), Emerging (Levels 1–2), Progressing (Levels 3–4), Challenge (Level 5 / exit-ready). The tier changes the sentence frame, not the task.
  • Alignment: functions map to the ELPA21 standards and Mississippi ELP Standards (listening, speaking, reading, writing across all 10 standards), and to the EL Progress reporting categories used under MS Accountability §15.

Daily use: post the week's function and sentence frames, embed them in whatever content students are already studying, and collect one oral and one written sample per cycle (see Assessment). Content teachers can use the same frames in general-ed classes.

Proficiency tiers: the same function at four levels

Every student does the same function each week; the tier sets the support, the sentence frame, and the expected output length.

Tier ELPA21 level What students can do with the function Support provided Expected output
Newcomer Level 1 (first weeks in US schools) Point, label, choose, copy a frame Pictures, word bank, gestures, L1 support, yes/no & either/or questions Single words to one completed frame
Emerging Levels 1–2 Complete and extend a frame with own words Sentence frames, word bank, partner talk, visuals 1–3 sentences
Progressing Levels 3–4 Produce the function in connected speech and paragraphs Sentence starters (not full frames), graphic organizers, academic word lists A paragraph oral or written; 5–8 sentences
Challenge Level 5 / exit-ready Use the function with grade-level text and register; vary structures Discourse-level supports only (models, rubrics) Multi-paragraph; extended discussion

Example — Function 4, Comparing (grade 6–8, science: solids vs. liquids)

  • Newcomer: A solid is ___. A liquid is ___. (word bank: hard, soft, flows, keeps shape)
  • Emerging: Solids and liquids both ___, but a solid ___ while a liquid ___.
  • Progressing: Although solids and liquids are both states of matter, they differ in ___ and ___. For example, …
  • Challenge: While solids and liquids share ___, the key distinction lies in ___, which explains why …

Moving between tiers: a student moves up when they produce the function at the next tier's output length on two consecutive quick checks without the frame. Tier placement is per function, not per student — a student can be Progressing in Describing and Emerging in Cause & Effect.

Quarter Plans

Quarter 1 (Weeks 1–9): Describing · Needs & preferences · Sequencing

Quarter 1 builds survival and classroom language: students can describe what they see, say what they need, and tell what happened in order.

Weeks 1–3 — Describing (ELPA21 Std 1, 3, 7)

Grade band Content context Core frames (Emerging → Progressing) Week 3 product
K–2 Self, family, classroom objects, animals This is a ___. It is ___ and ___. → My ___ has ___. It can ___. Draw-and-label + 3 spoken sentences about a classroom object
3–5 Habitats, communities, characters in a story The ___ is ___. It has ___. → The ___ is a ___ that ___. It lives/has/looks ___. Descriptive paragraph (5 sentences) about an animal or character
6–8 Cells, ecosystems, settings in literature, ancient civilizations ___ is characterized by ___. → ___ can be described as ___ because it ___. Science or social-studies description with 3 attributes and a diagram label
9–12 Lab specimens, historical figures, data sets, characters ___ consists of ___ and ___. → ___ exhibits ___, which is evident in ___. Technical description (one paragraph) of a text, figure, or artifact

Weeks 4–6 — Expressing needs, feelings & preferences (ELPA21 Std 2, 7)

Grade band Content context Core frames Week 6 product
K–2 Classroom routines, feelings chart, lunch, centers I need ___. I feel ___. I like ___. → I like ___ because ___. Oral: ask for 3 things in class using a full sentence
3–5 Group work roles, book choices, recess Can I have ___? I would rather ___. → I prefer ___ because ___. Short note to the teacher stating a need and a preference
6–8 Elective choices, project roles, help-seeking in class I need help with ___. → I'd prefer to ___ because ___. Could you clarify ___? Email-style request (4–5 sentences) to a teacher
9–12 Schedules, counselor meetings, job/college interests, lab partners I'm interested in ___. → I would like to ___; my reason is ___. Self-advocacy statement for a counselor/advisor meeting

Weeks 7–9 — Sequencing & retelling (ELPA21 Std 1, 3, 4)

Grade band Content context Core frames Week 9 product
K–2 Daily schedule, a familiar story, making a snack First ___. Then ___. Last ___. Retell a 3-event story with picture cards
3–5 Life cycles, story plot, a class experiment First… Next… After that… Finally… → Before ___, ___ happened. Retell of a story or procedure in 5+ ordered sentences
6–8 Historical timelines, digestion, lab procedure Initially… Subsequently… Eventually… → After ___ had ___, ___. Timeline narrative of a historical event (one paragraph)
9–12 Causes of a war, mitosis, a novel's plot, a lab report Prior to… Following… Ultimately… → Having ___, the ___ then ___. Sequenced summary of a chapter, process, or event (2 paragraphs)

Newcomer and Challenge frames for every function and band are on the Frame Ladder page.

Quarter 2 (Weeks 10–18): Comparing · Questioning · Cause & effect

Quarter 2 moves from describing one thing to relating two things: likeness, inquiry, and causation.

Weeks 10–12 — Comparing & contrasting (ELPA21 Std 1, 3, 9)

Grade band Content context Core frames (Emerging → Progressing) Week 12 product
K–2 Two animals, two shapes, two characters A ___ is big. A ___ is small. → Both ___ and ___ are ___, but ___ is ___. Venn diagram with 2 spoken sentences
3–5 Two habitats, two versions of a story, two states ___ and ___ are alike because ___. → ___ is ___er than ___. However, ___. Compare/contrast paragraph with both / but / however
6–8 Two civilizations, two characters, two text structures, fractions vs. decimals Similarly, ___. In contrast, ___. → While ___, ___ differs in that ___. Two-paragraph comparison with a T-chart
9–12 Two primary sources, two authors' styles, two functions in math, two cell types ___ parallels ___ in ___. → Whereas ___, ___; this distinction matters because ___. Analytical comparison (3 paragraphs) citing text evidence

Weeks 13–15 — Asking & answering questions (ELPA21 Std 2, 5, 7)

Grade band Content context Core frames Week 15 product
K–2 A read-aloud, a picture, a classmate What is ___? Where is ___? Is it ___? Ask a partner 3 questions about a picture and answer theirs
3–5 A nonfiction text, an experiment, a guest speaker Why did ___? How does ___? → I wonder ___. Can you explain ___? Interview a classmate; record 4 Q&A pairs
6–8 Research topic, text-dependent questions, lab inquiry What evidence shows ___? → How might ___ affect ___? What would happen if ___? Write 3 text-dependent questions and answer one with evidence
9–12 Socratic seminar, research question, clarifying a lecture Could you clarify what you meant by ___? → To what extent does ___? What are the implications of ___? Lead a 5-minute discussion with prepared open questions

Weeks 16–18 — Explaining cause & effect (ELPA21 Std 3, 4, 9)

Grade band Content context Core frames Week 18 product
K–2 Weather, plants, story events (why did the character cry?) ___ because ___. → When ___, ___. Picture pair: say what happened and why
3–5 Erosion, character actions, community changes ___ happened because ___. → Since ___, ___. As a result, ___. Cause-and-effect paragraph with 2 causes and 1 effect
6–8 Plate tectonics, causes of a conflict, chemical reactions ___ led to ___. → Due to ___, ___; consequently, ___. Cause-effect chain (3 links) explained in a paragraph
9–12 Economic causes of a revolution, feedback loops, literary motivation ___ resulted in ___. → The primary factor contributing to ___ was ___, which in turn ___. Analytical paragraph distinguishing immediate and underlying causes

Quarter 3 (Weeks 19–27): Instructions · Summarizing · Predicting & inferring

Quarter 3 shifts to academic processing: giving procedures, condensing text, and reasoning beyond what is stated.

Weeks 19–21 — Giving instructions & procedures (ELPA21 Std 2, 4, 10)

Grade band Content context Core frames (Emerging → Progressing) Week 21 product
K–2 How to wash hands, make a sandwich, play a game First, ___. Then, ___. Don't ___. Teach a partner a 3-step task with pictures
3–5 Science procedure, a math strategy, a craft To ___, you need ___. First… Next… Make sure you ___. Written how-to with materials list and 5 steps
6–8 Lab safety procedure, solving an equation, a recipe conversion Begin by ___. Be sure to ___ before ___. → If ___, then ___; otherwise, ___. Procedure text with conditional steps and a warning
9–12 Lab protocol, technical manual, peer-tutoring a math process The procedure requires ___. → Prior to ___, ensure that ___; failure to do so results in ___. Technical procedure (one page) a peer can follow without help

Weeks 22–24 — Summarizing & paraphrasing (ELPA21 Std 1, 5, 8)

Grade band Content context Core frames Week 24 product
K–2 A picture book, a short video, the school day This story is about ___. ___ happens. Oral 2-sentence summary with beginning and end
3–5 A chapter, a news article for kids, a science text The main idea is ___. One detail is ___. → In other words, ___. Summary paragraph: main idea + 3 details, no copied sentences
6–8 Textbook sections, speeches, short stories The text mainly argues ___. → The author claims ___, supporting it with ___ and ___. One-paragraph summary + one paraphrased key sentence
9–12 Research articles, primary sources, literary chapters The author's central claim is ___. → In essence, the author contends that ___, citing ___. Objective summary (150 words) with correct attribution verbs

Weeks 25–27 — Predicting & inferring (ELPA21 Std 1, 3, 8)

Grade band Content context Core frames Week 27 product
K–2 Story covers, weather, what a character will do I think ___ will ___. → I think ___ because I see ___. Predict before a read-aloud, check after; say if it was right
3–5 Character feelings, experiment outcomes, next chapter I predict ___ because ___. → The character probably feels ___ because the text says ___. Prediction + inference chart with text evidence
6–8 Theme, author's purpose, scientific hypothesis Based on ___, I infer ___. → The evidence suggests ___, which implies ___. Hypothesis statement + inference paragraph with 2 pieces of evidence
9–12 Tone, bias, data trends, historical outcomes It can be inferred that ___. → The data indicate a trend toward ___; one might reasonably anticipate ___. Evidence-based inference essay (2 paragraphs) distinguishing fact from inference

Quarter 4 (Weeks 28–36): Opinions · Agreeing & persuading · Evaluating & synthesizing

Quarter 4 is argument and judgment — the functions ELPA21 Levels 4–5 and grade-level assessments demand most.

Weeks 28–30 — Expressing & justifying opinions (ELPA21 Std 4, 6, 9)

Grade band Content context Core frames (Emerging → Progressing) Week 30 product
K–2 Favorite book/animal, best class pet, which game I like ___ best because ___. Oral opinion with one reason; draw and label
3–5 Should we have longer recess? Best character? In my opinion, ___. One reason is ___. → Another reason is ___. That's why ___. Opinion paragraph: claim + 2 reasons + closing
6–8 School policy, a character's choice, a scientific claim I believe ___ because ___. → The strongest reason is ___; for instance, ___. Claim + 2 reasons + text evidence (one page)
9–12 Social issue, literary interpretation, lab conclusion It is my position that ___. → I maintain that ___, given that ___ and ___. Argumentative paragraph with claim, evidence, and reasoning

Weeks 31–33 — Agreeing, disagreeing & persuading (ELPA21 Std 2, 4, 6)

Grade band Content context Core frames Week 33 product
K–2 Partner talk about a story, class vote I agree. / I don't agree. I think ___. Respond to a partner's opinion with agree/disagree + reason
3–5 Book club, class decision, persuasive letter I agree with ___ because ___. → I disagree because ___. You should ___ because ___. Persuasive letter (5+ sentences) to the principal or a parent
6–8 Structured debate, peer feedback, PSA I see your point, but ___. → Building on what ___ said, ___. Have you considered ___? 2-minute persuasive speech with a counterargument
9–12 Socratic seminar, policy debate, op-ed I respectfully disagree; ___. → While ___ raises a valid concern, the evidence favors ___ because ___. Op-ed or debate brief that answers one counterclaim

Weeks 34–36 — Evaluating & synthesizing (ELPA21 Std 1, 5, 8, 9)

Grade band Content context Core frames Week 36 product
K–2 Which story was better? What did we learn from two books? I think ___ is better because ___. Both books tell me ___. Choose between two texts and give a reason; one sentence combining them
3–5 Two sources on the same animal/event; rating a project Source A says ___. Source B says ___. Together they show ___. Mini-report combining 2 sources; rate a classmate's project with a rubric
6–8 Evaluating sources, lab conclusions, theme across texts The most reliable source is ___ because ___. → Taken together, the sources indicate ___. Synthesis paragraph from 3 sources with an evaluation of each
9–12 Research synthesis, literary criticism, evaluating arguments ___ is more credible than ___ owing to ___. → Synthesizing ___ and ___, it becomes clear that ___. Short research synthesis (2–3 pages) with evaluative commentary

Week 36 portfolio review: students choose one Q1, Q2, and Q3 product, redo each at their current tier, and explain (orally) one way their language grew. This is the year's single best piece of EL Progress evidence.

Assessment and evidence

Each 3-week cycle yields two scored samples per student — one oral, one written — so by week 36 every student has 24 dated pieces of evidence across all 12 functions.

Cycle rhythm

  1. Week 1, day 1: 2-minute oral pre-check (teacher prompt, student responds without a frame). Score the tier.
  2. Week 3: the product from the grade-band table (written) plus a 1–2 minute oral version (recorded or scored live).
  3. Score both on the 4-point function rubric below; record the tier on the student's function tracker.

Function rubric (same for every function; tier anchors the expectation)

Score Function accuracy Language control Independence
4 — Exceeds tier Function fully achieved with grade-level content Varied structures; errors don't obscure meaning No frame or bank used
3 — Meets tier Function achieved Tier's structures used correctly most of the time Frame used as a starter only
2 — Approaching Function partly achieved Relies on the frame; frequent errors Needs the full frame and word bank
1 — Beginning Function attempted; meaning unclear Single words or L1 Needs modeling and repetition

Mapping to ELPA21 domains — the oral sample covers Speaking (and Listening, since it answers a teacher prompt); the written product covers Writing; the source text or prompt covers Reading. One cycle = all four domains.

Documentation for compliance and portfolios

  • EL Progress (§15 / Title III): the function tracker (12 functions × tier score) is a direct, dated record of growth between ELPA21 administrations. Pair it with the ELPA21 scale score for the student's annual review.
  • Student LSP/ELSP: list the student's current tier per function and the accommodations tied to it (word bank, extended time, oral response).
  • NBCT Component 2/3 evidence: the week-3 oral recordings plus the rubric scores show instructional decisions and student growth over time with minimal extra work.
  • Content-teacher sharing: give general-ed teachers the week's function and the student's tier; they use the matching frame in class and return one scored sample per quarter.

Pacing notes

Late-entry newcomers. A student arriving in week 20 does not start at Function 7. Run them through a compressed Q1 (Functions 1–3, one week each) using the Newcomer frames, then join the class at the current function. Describing, needs, and sequencing are the survival functions; nothing later works without them.

90-minute blocks (secondary). Suggested split: 10 min function warm-up with the frame posted · 30 min content reading/listening in which the function appears · 30 min structured output (partner talk, then writing) · 10 min quick check · 10 min vocabulary and metacognition (which frame did you use, and why?). Elementary pull-out groups (30–45 min): warm-up, one input, one output.

Cross-content embedding. The function is the EL teacher's; the content is whatever the general-ed class is already doing. Each Monday, send content teachers the function name, the two core frames, and the signal words. The schedule also works as a staff-development sequence — one function per staff touchpoint matches a 36-week cadence.

Spiraling. Every function revisits the earlier ones: comparing uses describing; cause and effect uses sequencing; persuading uses opinions and questioning. If a cycle is lost to testing or weather, drop a week 2 practice week, never a week-3 product.

Testing windows. Keep Functions 9–11 (predicting, opinions, persuading) ahead of the spring ELPA21 window; they carry the most weight in the Level 4–5 speaking and writing tasks.

Function 1 — Describing

Text: The Armadillo

The nine-banded armadillo is a small mammal that lives in Mississippi and across the southern United States. It is about the size of a house cat. Its body is covered with hard, bony plates that look like armor. The plates are gray or brown, and they protect the armadillo from thorns and some enemies. The armadillo has a long, pointed nose and small black eyes. Its tail is long and covered with rings of the same hard material.

Armadillos have strong front legs with sharp claws. They use their claws to dig for ants, beetles, and worms. They are active mostly at night, so people rarely see them during the day. An armadillo cannot see well, but it has an excellent sense of smell. When it is scared, it jumps straight up in the air. Because of this habit, many armadillos are hit by cars on country roads.

Function 2 — Expressing needs, feelings & preferences

Text: Asking for What You Need at School

Every student needs help sometimes. A new student might need to know where the bathroom is. Another student might need more time to finish a test. Some students need a quiet place to work, and some prefer to work with a partner. Knowing how to say what you need is an important school skill.

There are polite ways to express a need. You can say, "I need a pencil, please," or "Can I have more time?" If you are confused, you can say, "I don't understand. Can you explain it again?" Teachers want students to ask. They cannot help with a problem they do not know about.

Feelings matter too. If you feel nervous before a test, you can tell your teacher. If you feel proud of your work, you can share it. Saying how you feel helps other people understand you. It also helps you make choices. A student who says, "I prefer to read first and then write," is telling the teacher how they learn best.

Function 3 — Sequencing & retelling

Text: How a Monarch Butterfly Grows

A monarch butterfly changes its body four times during its life. First, a female monarch lays a tiny egg on a milkweed leaf. The egg is about the size of the head of a pin. After three or four days, a small caterpillar hatches from the egg.

Next, the caterpillar eats milkweed leaves almost all day. It grows quickly. In about two weeks, it becomes two thousand times heavier than when it hatched. Then the caterpillar hangs upside down from a leaf and forms a hard green case called a chrysalis.

Inside the chrysalis, the caterpillar's body slowly changes. After about ten days, the case cracks open. Finally, an orange and black butterfly comes out. Its wings are wet and soft at first. After a few hours, the wings dry and become strong, and the monarch flies away to find flowers. In the fall, many monarchs fly thousands of miles south to Mexico.

Function 4 — Comparing & contrasting

Text: Alligators and Crocodiles

Alligators and crocodiles look so much alike that many people mix them up. Both are large reptiles with long bodies, strong tails, and sharp teeth. Both lay eggs, and both spend time in water and on land. However, there are clear differences between them.

One difference is the shape of the head. An alligator has a wide, rounded snout shaped like the letter U. A crocodile has a narrower, pointed snout shaped like the letter V. Another difference is the teeth. When an alligator closes its mouth, its lower teeth are hidden. When a crocodile closes its mouth, a large lower tooth sticks up on each side.

The two animals also live in different places. Alligators live in fresh water, such as rivers, swamps, and lakes in Mississippi and other southern states. Crocodiles can live in salt water as well as fresh water. In the United States, crocodiles live only in the southern tip of Florida. Alligators are usually darker in color, while crocodiles are lighter, often tan or olive. Both animals are powerful hunters, but crocodiles are generally more aggressive than alligators.

Function 5 — Asking & answering questions

Text: A Visit from a Firefighter

Last week a firefighter named Ms. Ortega visited our school. Before she came, our class wrote questions. Good questions begin with words like who, what, when, where, why, and how. We also learned that some questions can be answered with "yes" or "no," and others need a longer answer.

Marcus asked, "What do you do when you are not fighting fires?" Ms. Ortega said firefighters spend most of their time training, checking equipment, and visiting schools. Ana asked, "Why do you wear such heavy clothes?" Ms. Ortega explained that the coat and pants are made of special material that does not burn. The air tank on her back weighs about 30 pounds.

Function 6 — Explaining cause & effect

Text: Why Rivers Flood

Mississippi has many rivers, and sometimes they flood. A flood happens when more water comes into a river than the river can hold. Heavy rain is the most common cause. When rain falls for many days, the ground becomes soaked and cannot take in any more water. As a result, the extra water runs into streams and rivers, and the water level rises.

People can also make floods worse. When trees and plants are cut down, there are fewer roots to hold the soil and soak up water. Because of this, rain runs off the land faster. Roads and parking lots have the same effect: water cannot sink into concrete, so it flows straight into the river.

Floods have many effects. Water can cover roads, so people cannot drive to work or school. It can enter homes and damage furniture and walls. Crops in low fields can be destroyed. On the other hand, floods leave behind rich soil, which is why many farms are built near rivers. Engineers build levees, which are long walls of earth, to keep rivers from flooding towns.

Function 7 — Giving instructions & procedures

Text: How to Grow a Bean Plant in a Cup

You can grow a bean plant in your classroom in about two weeks. You will need a clear plastic cup, a paper towel, three dried beans, water, and a sunny window.

First, fold the paper towel and wet it with water. It should be damp, not dripping. Next, press the towel against the inside of the cup. Then place the beans between the towel and the side of the cup so you can see them. Make sure the beans do not touch the bottom, where extra water collects.

After that, put the cup in a sunny window. Check the towel every day. If it feels dry, add a little water. Do not add too much, or the beans will rot. In three to five days, a small white root will push out of each bean. A few days later, a green shoot will grow upward toward the light.

Finally, when the plant has two leaves, move it to a pot of soil. Keep a daily log: write the date and describe what you see. If a bean does not sprout after ten days, throw it away and try another.

Function 8 — Summarizing & paraphrasing

Text: Why Sleep Matters

Many students think sleep is wasted time, but scientists say the opposite. During sleep, the brain sorts the information it took in during the day. It moves important facts into long-term memory and clears out what it does not need. That is why a student who studies and then sleeps well usually remembers more than a student who stays up all night.

Sleep also helps the body. While you sleep, your muscles repair themselves and your body fights germs. Children and teenagers who do not get enough sleep get sick more often and have a harder time paying attention in class. Doctors recommend 9 to 12 hours of sleep for children ages 6 to 12, and 8 to 10 hours for teenagers.

Screens are one of the biggest problems. The light from phones and tablets tells the brain it is still daytime, so it is harder to fall asleep. Experts suggest turning off screens at least one hour before bed, keeping the same bedtime every night, and keeping the bedroom dark and cool. Small changes like these can improve grades, mood, and health.

Function 12 — Evaluating & synthesizing

Text: Two Reports on Plastic Bags

Sometimes two sources talk about the same topic but say different things. A careful reader looks at both, decides which is more reliable, and combines what they learn.

Source A is a post on a shopping website. It says plastic bags are the best choice for shoppers because they are cheap, light, and strong. The post does not say who wrote it, and it gives no numbers. It ends with a link to buy bags.

Source B is a 2024 report from a state environmental agency. It says that Americans use about 100 billion plastic bags each year and that less than 10 percent are recycled. It explains that bags that are not recycled end up in landfills, rivers, and oceans, where they can harm fish and birds. The report was written by three scientists and lists the studies it used.

A strong reader would say that Source B is more credible because it names its authors, gives data, and shows where the data came from. Source A has a reason to sell bags. But Source A is not useless: it shows why many shoppers still choose plastic. Putting the two together, a reader could conclude that plastic bags are convenient for shoppers but costly for the environment, and that any solution has to deal with both facts.
