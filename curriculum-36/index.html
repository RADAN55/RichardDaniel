A research-defensible 36-week curriculum for a combined Emerging/Progressing 9–12 block in Mississippi should run one shared theme calendar with two tiered language lanes — the pattern Harford County uses for ESOL I and II, where both courses keep identical units and week counts while grammar and text types escalate (HCPS ESOL I; HCPS ESOL II) — anchored to the ten CCSSO/ELPA21 ELP Standards and built backward from the ELPT window of March 1 – April 16, 2027 (MDE 2026–27 Testing Calendar). The eight units below follow the sequence that four independent newcomer maps converge on (self and school → life domains → narrative past → content-area language → argument → research → future), while the Emerging lane climbs the Standard 10 verb-tense ladder (present/going-to/simple past at Level 1; irregular past, past progressive, future at Level 2) and the Progressing lane climbs the Level 3–4 rungs (present perfect, past perfect, complex phrases and clauses) (Oregon ODE, ELP Standards 9–12). Only a handful of instructional ingredients carry strong experimental evidence — intensive academic vocabulary anchored in short informational texts, daily structured pair/small-group talk about content, and peer-mediated learning generally (IES/WWC; Cole 2012) — so the 90-minute block template puts those three at its center and treats the teacher-led 3–5-student group (moderate evidence) as the daily home for newcomers. The week-by-week map rotates every ELPA21 task family from the grades 9–12 blueprint (26 reading, 23 listening, 6 speaking, 5 writing items) so that the Monday-to-Thursday mirror tasks accumulate into quarterly four-domain benchmarks timed to the state's required quarterly LSP/SET reviews (Oregon ODE, ELPA21 Test Specs 9–12; MDE 2016 EL Guide). The honest caveats: no experimental study tests a 90-minute mixed-level secondary ELD block, the National Academies report explicitly declines to rate practices for grades 9–12, the Laurel 2026–27 calendar was not retrievable, and every mdek12.org document returned 403 — so the Mississippi policy citations below come from MDE documents mirrored on district sites and should be reconciled against the current MDE EL Guidelines before being quoted as policy.

Evidence tags used throughout: R-strong / R-moderate / R-minimal = formally rated experimental evidence (WWC, Saunders et al., meta-analysis); R-hyp = guideline the authors themselves rate as hypothesis-level or non-EL-derived; PC = practitioner consensus across frameworks or district maps; INF = this report's own inference or design choice.

Accountability frame: exit depends on the weakest domain, growth on the overall score

Everything in this map answers to two scoring rules. First, ELPA21 declares a student Proficient only when all four domains reach Level 4 or 5; Emerging means all four domains at Levels 1–2, and Progressing is every other combination (Oregon ODE, ELPA21 Proficiency Descriptors). A Mississippi district plan restating MDE policy confirms the same rule and adds that exited students are monitored for at least four years with a SET review every nine weeks (Meridian PSD EL Plan 2025–26). Because the 9–12 writing and reading cut scores sit highest (Writing Level 4 begins at 615, Reading at 631, versus Listening 571 and Speaking 593), a Progressing student is most often held back by writing or reading, which is why the Progressing lane in this map front-loads paragraph and essay work and rotates discrete editing tasks every unit (Nebraska DOE ALDs 9–12). Second, the §15 EL Progress component — 5% of the accountability model — is computed from the ELPT overall scale score: annual goal = (proficiency cut at year 5 − prior-year score) ÷ years remaining, with progress rate bounded between 0 and 1 (MS Accountability Business Rules). The practical implication INF is that each student's LSP goal in Ellevation should be expressed as a scale-score target on a five-year trajectory, not just a level label, and that the Emerging lane earns the school just as many progress points as the Progressing lane when it moves students one-fifth of the way to the cut.

The standards themselves are the CCSSO 2014 ELP Standards adopted by every ELPA21 state, with Standards 1–7 as "content-specific practices" and 8–10 as the "micro-level linguistic features" that serve them (Oregon ODE, ELP 9–12 with ELA correspondences). For walkthrough posting, the same CCSSO document supplies the MS CCRS ELA correspondence (Mississippi uses CCSS numbering): ELP 1 ↔ RL/RI.1, 2, 3, 7 and SL.2; ELP 2 ↔ W.6, SL.1; ELP 3 ↔ W.2, W.3, SL.4; ELP 4 ↔ W.1, SL.4, L.6; ELP 5 ↔ W.7–9; ELP 6 ↔ RI.8, W.1b, SL.3; ELP 7 ↔ W.5, SL.6, L.6; ELP 8 ↔ RL/RI.4, L.4–5; ELP 9 ↔ W.4 and the c-clauses of W.1–3; ELP 10 ↔ L.1, L.3 (Oregon ODE correspondences). The map cites these at the 9–10 level; for juniors and seniors the same standard numbers apply at 11–12, but the 11–12 correspondence table itself was not retrieved. The ELPA21 blueprint tells you where the points are: Reading is 49% Standard 1 and 23% Standard 5; Listening is 53% Standard 1; Speaking is 88% Standard 3; Writing is 31% Standard 2, 25% Standard 10, 18% Standard 7 (ELPA21 Test Specs 9–12). That weighting is why the mirror-task column below leans toward comprehension of multi-turn academic talk and short informational sets, "speak about a text or visual" rather than social conversation, peer-email responses, and sentence-level editing.

The LSP and SET cadence gives the map its fixed assessment posts. Mississippi expects an LSP with "measurable goals for listening, speaking, reading, and writing," program minutes, classroom and testing accommodations, and ELP score history, developed by the SET at the start of the year and adjusted during it (MDE Parent and Guardian Guide 2020); MDE's 2016 guide says the SET "should meet at least quarterly" (MDE 2016 EL Guide), and district plans operationalize that as a quarterly LSP evaluation (Lowndes County SD EL Plan). Screening runs within 30 calendar days for start-of-year enrollees and within 10 school days for mid-year arrivals, and the ELPA21 Dynamic Screener is open on DRC INSIGHT from July 20, 2026 through May 27, 2027 (Lowndes EL Plan; MDE 2026–27 Testing Calendar). One accommodation rule shapes classroom routine from Week 1: the 2017 MTAM allows a word-to-word native-language dictionary (#81) and reading directions only (#53) on ELPT but prohibits reading or paraphrasing test items (#55, #58), whereas English II permits read-aloud, extended time, and multiple sessions — and all accommodations must be "used during routine classroom instruction" to be defensible (MTAM Nov 2017, district copy). A February 2026 MTAM revision exists but could not be opened, so treat the numbering as provisional.

What the evidence actually supports, and what is consensus or inference

The instructional core rests on four rated recommendations. The WWC practice guide gives strong evidence (six studies, five RCTs) to teaching a small set of academic words intensively over several days, anchored in a brief informational text and practiced across modalities, and strong evidence (five RCTs) to integrating oral and written English into content instruction with "daily opportunities for students to talk about content in pairs or small groups" (IES/WWC). It gives moderate evidence to small-group intervention of 3–5 students for ELs struggling in literacy, and only minimal evidence to structured writing with sentence starters and organizers (IES/WWC). Cole's meta-analysis of 44 studies found peer-mediated learning "effective for ELLs across every outcome type analyzed" (Vanderbilt), and Saunders, Goldenberg and Marcelletti rate as "relatively strong" only two of their fourteen ELD guidelines: that providing ELD at all helps (focused instruction yielding "more than five times the gains") and that interactive tasks work when the target form is essential and output is required (Saunders et al. 2013). Those same authors rate grouping by proficiency and writing language objectives as "applicable but grounded in non-EL research," and a separate daily ELD block, explicit teaching of forms, a ~60/40 oral-to-literacy split, strategic L1 use, and prompt-based corrective feedback as "hypothesis" level (Saunders et al. 2013). The honest frame for a principal is therefore: the vocabulary routine, the partner talk, and the small group are research-backed; the posted language objectives and the block itself are professionally sensible but unproven as such.

Two caveats specific to high school matter. The WWC guide's scope is elementary and middle school, and the 2017 National Academies report states it "provides minimal specific practices guidance for high school ELs," calls high school a major research gap, and finds engagement measures "virtually absent" from intervention studies (NASEM ch. 8). SIOP's flagship RCT does not meet WWC standards because of attrition and baseline non-equivalence (WWC study page). What is documented for adolescents is sobering program data: in Montgomery County's secondary SLIFE program, only 30% of Level 1 starters reached Level 2 and 19.9% reached Level 3 in a year, staff rated existing curricula "too linguistically complex" and lacking phonics materials for older emergent readers, and 62% of program exits were "lost contact/disengaged" (MCPS SLIFE evaluation 2025). Browder's study of 165 high-school SLIFE found that perceived social distance predicted test achievement better than academic self-concept (Browder 2014). Together these justify INF treating the heterogeneous partner structures as the engagement intervention, not just a language one, and setting a realistic one-year expectation of roughly one level of growth for Level 1 starters.

The sequence of themes, grammar, and genres is practitioner consensus rather than experiment, but it is a strikingly consistent consensus. Lincoln-Way's 2024–25 Newcomer English (12 units × 4 weeks), Harford's ESOL I/II (7 units, 38 weeks), St Paul's Stage 5 Beginner, and the Ventures series all open with self/school/personal information, place food, health, community and weather in the first half, and back-load content-area language and goals/future (Lincoln-Way 210; HCPS ESOL I; St Paul's; Ventures 3e Basic). Their grammar order converges on be and pronouns → prepositions of place → plurals and there is/are → yes/no and Wh-questions → simple present with frequency adverbs → present continuous → prepositions of time → simple past (be, then regular/irregular) → can → going to/will → modals → comparatives → requests → clauses with and/but/because → sequence and procedural language (Ventures 3e Level 1; Ventures 3e Level 2; HCPS ESOL II). Lincoln-Way does not reach past tense until Unit 7 of 12, roughly the 60% mark (Lincoln-Way 210); this map pulls it forward to Week 14 INF because the Progressing lane needs narrative past for the Storyboard task and because ELP 10 lists simple past at Level 1. Writing genres likewise move from forms and labels → sentences → descriptive and schedule paragraphs → past-tense narrative → informational/how-to/lab report → opinion and argument → research, in both district maps and the Edge anthology (Edge Program Overview; Lincoln-Way 210). Writing Next's meta-analysis ranks writing strategies, summarization, collaborative writing, product goals, and sentence combining as the most effective elements, though it does not disaggregate ELs (AdLit summary of Writing Next); those five elements are built into the Thursday writing day.

The 36-week scope and sequence

Calendar assumptions [INF, unverified for Laurel]. Week 1 = August 3–7, 2026 and Week 11 = October 12–16, per the reader's count. Weeks are numbered continuously through the Thanksgiving week (treated as a short week) and skip winter break (Dec 21–Jan 1) and spring break (Mar 29–Apr 2), using the generic Mississippi 2026–27 pattern of an August 3 start, May 21 finish, and 180 days (us-school-calendar.com Mississippi). Under that count Week 36 ends April 30, and the three remaining calendar weeks (May 3–21) form a closeout tail that absorbs the English II window, final portfolios, LSP annual updates, and the slippage any real year produces. Laurel's published reference to "intersession schedules" means its quarter boundaries may differ (Laurel SD 2025–26 calendar announcement); shift rows, not content, once the district PDF is in hand. State dates are from the MDE 2026–27 calendar: ELPT March 1–April 16; MAAP EOC (English II) April 26–May 21; fall EOC retest Nov 30–Dec 18; ACT grade-11 events Feb 23, Mar 23, Apr 20; ACT WorkKeys Oct 19–Nov 13 and Mar 1–26; NAEP Science Jan 25–Mar 19 for selected schools (MDE 2026–27 Testing Calendar).

The Lexile staircase and words-per-week figures are design targets, not research findings: MetaMetrics' matching rule is 100L below to 50L above a reader's measure for roughly 75% comprehension (MetaMetrics FAQs), but Shanahan argues that grade-level text "punctuated with somewhat easier ones," with explicit vocabulary, syntax and background support, produces larger gains than leveled text for everyone except true beginning readers (Education Next). Every unit therefore keeps one grade-band anchor text for all lanes, accessed by read-aloud, audio, chunking, and tiered versions.

Three design choices in this table are the report's own inferences and deserve explanation. First, Unit 4 spans seven weeks because the narrative/novel work tolerates the holiday-fragmented November–December stretch better than a new informational unit would, and because the Storyboard writing task and Short/Extended Literary reading sets together carry substantial blueprint weight (ELPA21 Test Specs 9–12). Second, Unit 7 is deliberately a project with low daily prep and station-friendly structure so that instruction survives the six-week ELPT window during which students leave the room in domain sessions; the ELPT is untimed with an estimated 155 minutes for grades 9–12, so schools stage it across days (NeMTSS ELPA21 overview). Third, argument (Unit 6) lands in February, immediately before the test, because Construct a Claim, Respond to Peer Email, Academic Debate, and the Argument and Support Essay Set are the task families the Progressing lane most needs to have fresh and because ELP 4 and 6 at Levels 3–4 ("sufficient reasons," "analyze the reasoning and use of rhetoric") are the descriptors that separate Level 3 from Level 4 writing (Oregon ODE, ELP Standards 9–12).

The literacy strand: contrastive phonics for the few, cognates and morphology for all

The strand runs in parallel with the theme calendar rather than pausing it, which is how every publisher handles it (Edge Fundamentals' "Inside Phonics," Bridges' separate Foundational Language and Literacy course, NGL's Stand Out Literacy Foundations) (Edge Program Overview; NYSED SIFE brief; NGL K12 Newcomer). Its first job is triage PC: a Week 1–2 diagnostic that separates Spanish-literate newcomers, who transfer decoding concepts and need a short contrastive course, from non-literate adolescents, who need the full foundational sequence through age-appropriate materials. Cárdenas-Hagan's guidance is to teach phonological awareness explicitly but contrastively, "identifying sounds that transfer across language," and to keep decoding integrated with meaning (Colorín Colorado). The non-transferring inventory for Spanish speakers is specific: short /ă/ /ĭ/ /ŭ/, schwa, r-controlled vowels, vowel teams /oo/ /aw/, consonants /v/ /z/ /j/ /r/, digraphs sh/ng/th/wh/zh, and s-blends (Colorín Colorado handout); Spanish has five vowel sounds against roughly twenty in English, and each Spanish vowel letter maps to one sound, so the explicit message to literate newcomers is that the letters are the same but "reading in English is more complicated," with minimal pairs such as bit/beat taught with visuals and meaning (Reading Universe). The ordering of that course in the map's Newcomer small group — transferring letters as a quick check (Wk 1–2), short vowels (Wk 3–6), /v/ /z/ /j/ and English /r/ (Wk 7–10), digraphs (Wk 11–13), s-blends and the epenthetic "e-school" habit (Wk 14–16), r-controlled vowels and vowel teams (Wk 17–24), schwa and multisyllabic decoding through cognate suffixes (Wk 25–36) — is the report's synthesis INF; no source gives an order, and no peer-reviewed study tests a contrastive sequence with high-school newcomers. For the SLIFE minority, the full sequence runs in the same daily slot with hi-lo decodables, consistent with WWC's moderate-evidence small-group recommendation and CAL's finding that literacy background is "the most important factor" in newcomer program design (CAL newcomer summary). Shanahan's caution bounds the strand: older struggling readers are generally not beginning readers, and those below the "decoding threshold" struggle with multisyllabic and morphologically complex words they will never meet in below-grade texts (Education Next).

Cognates and morphology are the strand's engine for everyone, and here the evidence is better. In a study of 90 Spanish-speaking ELs, Spanish derivational awareness predicted English cognate vocabulary (explaining 13% of variance) but not noncognate vocabulary, and fed English reading comprehension indirectly; the authors recommend teaching morphological and cognate strategies together and note derivational awareness "can be assessed in either English or Spanish" — a usable intake screen for L1 literacy (Cross-Linguistic Transfer PDF). Carlo et al.'s intervention teaching context, morphology, polysemy, and cognate strategies produced gains for ELs "as large as for the English-only speakers" (Colorín Colorado summary of Carlo et al.), and CAL's CREATE secondary science work found about 88% of key science words were Spanish cognates and taught roughly five words per lesson with bilingual definitions (CAL CREATE briefs). The suffix correspondences to teach first are documented (-ción/-tion, -tad/-dad/-ty, -ez/-ity or -ness, -oso/-ous, -cio/-cia/-nce, -mento/-ment), as are identical cognates (hospital, doctor, ideal) and false friends (pariente = relative) (UW Spanish Reading OER); the map's unit-by-unit progression from identical cognates to suffix pairs to Latin prefixes to Greek/Latin roots is this report's ordering INF. Weekly vocabulary load follows WWC's "five to eight words from the text" taught across several days through writing, speaking, and listening (Nebraska DoE summary of WWC Rec. 1) plus about five content words, for roughly 10–13 explicitly taught words per week INF — sorted each Monday into transparent cognates (teach fast, use for morphology), partial or false cognates (contrast explicitly), and noncognates (full multi-day routine). The Academic Vocabulary List is the better master list than the AWL: its 570-family core covers about 14% of academic text versus about 7% for the AWL, and it does not exclude common academic words such as company and market (EAP Foundation). Lesaux's ALIAS program shows what an academic-vocabulary curriculum with daily writing can do at scale — 2,082 sixth graders, nine two-week units, gains in vocabulary, morphological awareness, expository comprehension, and written language, with "effects generally larger" for non-English-home students (UC Davis) — and its two-week, nine-day cycle is a reasonable model for the Progressing lane's word work.

Comprehension routines complete the strand. Collaborative Strategic Reading has middle-school experimental support and worked better in ELA than in reading-intervention classes (Meadows Center), which is why the Progressing lane's Tuesday reading uses CSR's preview, click-and-clunk, gist, and wrap-up while the Emerging lane uses read-aloud plus partner reread INF. Text sets should be sequenced from introduction to elaboration to consequences and mix articles, video, infographics, and literary pieces so vocabulary recurs across sources (TESOL text-sets blog). For monitoring, Shanahan's caution applies: maze and ORF measure sentence-level processing and speed, not comprehension growth, so use them as screeners for the Newcomer lane and instead track the Lexile of graded passages students can handle each quarter (Shanahan on Literacy); DIBELS 8 materials stop at grade 8, so any high-school use is off-level and unnormed (DIBELS 8 Admin Guide).

The 90-minute block and the five-day cycle

No source provides a research-validated minute-by-minute ELD block; the template below is a synthesis INF that places the strong-evidence ingredients in the longest slots and borrows its shape from Manitoba's "hourglass" workshop (whole-class mini-lesson → regroup → teacher-led flexible group while others work independently → conferencing → closure), the only concrete multilevel time structure located (Manitoba ch. 4). The oral-heavy split reflects Saunders' hypothesis-level ~60/40 guideline (Saunders et al. 2013).

Explicit device onboarding in Weeks 1–2 is not optional: with low-literate adult newcomers, most Chromebook barriers "happen at the commencement of the program," and scaffolded instruction moved learners from emergent to competent users (LESLLA proceedings).

For walkthroughs, post one content objective and two language objectives tied to the same function, one per lane, with can-do statements lifted from the Level 1–2 and Level 3–4 descriptors of the targeted standard; that mirrors Gibbons' principle of holding cognitive demand constant while varying the scaffold (Gibbons ch. 1). A worked example for Week 11 (health advice): MS CCRS: W.9-10.1, SL.9-10.6. MS ELP: Standards 2, 4, 7. Content objective: Students will explain a health problem and recommend a response using evidence from a clinic infographic. Language objective (Emerging): I can describe a symptom and give advice using "I have " and "You should ." Language objective (Progressing): I can write an advice email that states a claim, gives two reasons, and uses a first-conditional sentence ("If you , you should "). Can-do (L1–2, ELP 4): express an opinion about a familiar topic using formulaic expressions and modeled sentences; Can-do (L3–4, ELP 4): construct a claim, provide sufficient reasons, and provide a concluding sentence (Oregon ODE, ELP Standards 9–12).

Assessment and pacing calendar

The assessment system has three layers that feed one another INF: a weekly one-domain exit artifact, a four-domain unit check every 4–5 weeks, and a four-domain benchmark at each quarter boundary that supplies the evidence the SET's quarterly LSP review requires. For scoring, the only public ELPA21 rubric is the screener's 0–2 speaking rubric (2 = meaning clear despite some errors; 1 = frequent errors that may significantly interfere; 0 = refusal, non-English, or single-word yes/no) (ELPA Screener Speaking Scoring); no summative writing rubric was found, so classroom writing rubrics must be derived from the five-level proficiency descriptors and the domain cut scores (ELPA Performance Standards). The workable scheme is to score every weekly speaking and writing artifact twice — 0–2 for task completion and a Level 1–5 band from the descriptors — and store both in Flashlight360 so quarterly benchmarks produce a per-domain level trajectory that Ellevation's LSP goals can be checked against. Grading itself is a gap: MDE's 2016 guide "does not address EL-specific grading policies," so a growth-based grade built on domain movement, task-completion scores, and a portfolio tied to LSP goals is the most defensible design but not a state-mandated one (MDE 2016 EL Guide).

Gaps and what to verify before publishing

The evidence base is thinnest exactly where this curriculum lives. No experimental study tests a 90-minute mixed-level secondary ELD block, the WWC guide stops at middle school, and NASEM declines to rate high-school practices (NASEM ch. 8); SIOP's flagship trial fails WWC standards (WWC); station rotation evidence is EFL, not U.S. secondary ESL; and Chromebook practice evidence is elementary or adult. Multilevel frameworks (Bell, CAL, Manitoba) are practitioner-derived, and no study compares "same text, different task" with "different text, same task." Motivation and identity-text claims are correlational or theoretical. There is no evidence base for whole-class children's novels with high-school ELs; no source addresses a primary-grade novel with secondary newcomers, and the indirect evidence (adolescent-appropriate basic-literacy materials, hi-lo publishers' age-respect argument, Shanahan's grade-level-text principle) points toward a hi-lo or YA text with audio and a Spanish edition rather than a primary-grade book (CAL; Education Next). Writing Next effect sizes and the CCSS Lexile stretch bands were not verified in the fetched sources and are deliberately not quoted here.

On the Mississippi side, every mdek12.org document — the May 2018 EL Guidelines, the February 2026 MTAM, the Elementary Education and Federal Programs EL pages, the state-hosted ELP Standards — returned 403, so policy citations rely on MDE's 2016 guide and 2020 parent guide as mirrored by districts and on Meridian's and Lowndes' district plans. Those plans differ in how they state the exit rule (Meridian: Level 4+ in all four domains; Lowndes 2019: overall 4–5 plus reading and writing 4–5), which is a reason to confirm the current rule with MDE's EL coordinator before it is written into an LSP template (Meridian PSD EL Plan; Lowndes EL Plan). The Laurel 2026–27 calendar, its intersession structure, quarter end dates, and the Thanksgiving-week closure were not found, so every date in the map after Week 11 is generic Mississippi. The 2021–22 approved-courses manual showed no ESL course codes carrying credit, and the 2025–26 manual was not located (Approved Courses 2021–22). The grades 11–12 ELP/ELA correspondence table, the ELPA21 screener blueprint for 9–12, the Mississippi domain administration order on DRC INSIGHT, the ELPT results-release date, and any Flashlight360 or Ellevation vendor guidance on monitoring cadence were not retrievable. Finally, the 2025 accountability "revamp" reported in the press may alter the 5% EL weight or the five-year target (Mississippi Free Press); the business rules cited here predate it.

Conclusion

The most useful reframing this synthesis offers is that a combined Emerging/Progressing block is not two curricula taught at once but one theme calendar with a two-rung ladder at every point where language is specified — grammar, text version, writing length, and can-do statement — and that the ladder's rungs are already written in the ELP Standards' Level 1–2 versus Level 3–4 descriptors. Read that way, the standards document doubles as the differentiation plan, the walkthrough posting template, and the rubric source, which collapses three administrative demands into one. The second shift is in what counts as evidence for the design: the block's architecture (vocabulary anchored in short informational text, daily structured partner talk, a guaranteed daily small group for newcomers) is where the strong and moderate evidence lives, while the parts that look most like "curriculum" — the unit order, the grammar sequence, the genre progression — are consensus engineering that should be presented to administrators as such.

Going forward, the map's own assessment layer is the research the field lacks. Because the ELPT is scored on an overall scale and §15 rewards one-fifth-of-the-gap growth per year, a teacher who logs Flashlight360 artifacts with 0–2 and level-band scores against each unit's mirror tasks will, by Benchmark 4, hold a per-domain trajectory for every student that no published study of grade 9–12 ELD blocks currently provides — exactly the kind of growth evidence an NBCT portfolio or a district coordinator's program review needs. The map should therefore be treated as a hypothesis with its measurement built in, and the Week 20 and Week 28 benchmarks as the points at which the lane assignments, the novel choice, and the pace of the grammar ladder get revised on data rather than defended on principle.
